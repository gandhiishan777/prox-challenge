# Welding Tips and Weld Diagnosis

<!-- page: om-34 -->

## Welding Tips

A good way to test welding technique is to examine a weld's appearance after it has cooled and the slag has been removed. Then, better welding can be learned by adjusting your weld technique to remedy any problems found.

*NOTICE:* TIG welding is a complicated process, requiring experience and skill to achieve successful results. Training beyond the scope of this manual is required to TIG weld properly.

> After practice welding a couple of welding beads, STOP and examine your weld using the following guidelines.

## Strike Test

**A test weld on a PIECE OF SCRAP can be tested by using the following procedure. WEAR ANSI-APPROVED SAFETY GOGGLES DURING THIS PROCEDURE.**

**CAUTION!** This test WILL damage the weld it is performed on. This test is ONLY an indicator of weld technique and is not intended to test working welds.

1. After two scraps have been welded together and the weld has cooled, clamp one scrap in a sturdy vise.
2. Stay clear from underneath while you strike the opposite scrap with a heavy hammer, preferably a dead-blow hammer.
3. A **GOOD WELD** will deform but not break, as shown on top.
   A **POOR WELD** will be brittle and snap at the weld, as shown on bottom.

## Cleaning the Weld

> **WARNING:** TO PREVENT SERIOUS INJURY: Continue to wear ANSI-approved safety goggles and protective wear when cleaning a weld. Sparks or chips may fly when cleaning.

[FIGURE warning-safety-goggles-icon | bbox: 133,1573,270,1660 | caption: Safety icon depicting a person's head wearing safety goggles, reinforcing the requirement to wear ANSI-approved eye protection when cleaning a weld.]
- Icon: silhouette of a head wearing goggles, no additional text within the icon.

1. A weld from flux-cored wire welding or stick welding will be covered by slag. Use a chipping hammer to knock this off. **Be careful not to damage the weld or base material.**
2. Use a wire brush to further clean the weld or use an angle grinder (sold separately) to shape the weld.

[FIGURE chipping-hammer-wire-brush | bbox: 850,1518,1550,1900 | caption: Illustration of a combination tool with a chipping hammer point on one end and a wire brush on the other end, used to clean slag from a weld.]
- Label: "Chipping Hammer" — pointing to the pointed metal tip at the upper end of the tool.
- Label: "Wire Brush" — pointing to the bristled brush head at the lower/right end of the tool.
- The tool is shown as a single elongated handle with the chipping hammer point at the top and the wire brush bristles extending diagonally toward the bottom right.

# Wire Weld Penetration (Workpiece Heat Control)

[FIGURE excess-penetration-profile | bbox: 108,205,610,600 | Profile view of a weld cross-section showing excess penetration/burn-through, where the weld droops on top and underneath or falls through entirely, making a hole.]

[FIGURE proper-penetration-profile | bbox: 635,205,1050,600 | Profile view of a weld cross-section showing proper penetration, where the weld is visible underneath and bulges slightly on top.]
- Heading above figure: "PROPER PENETRATION"

- Drawing: two gray rectangular workpiece halves with a moderate dark teardrop shape centered at the joint, bulging slightly above the surface and showing a small even bead underneath.
- Caption below drawing (shared across all three profile figures): "PROFILE VIEWS"

[FIGURE inadequate-penetration-profile | bbox: 1075,205,1520,600 | Profile view of a weld cross-section showing inadequate penetration, where the weld does not penetrate the joint fully and sits only on the surface.]
- Heading above figure: "INADEQUATE PENETRATION"
- Sub-caption: "Weld does not penetrate the joint fully, just on the surface."
- Drawing: two gray rectangular workpiece halves with a narrow gap between them and a small dark lens-shaped bead sitting only on the top surface, not filling the gap below.
- Below the drawing, a bold black arrow points down-and-left toward the "POSSIBLE CAUSES AND SOLUTIONS" list for this condition.

### POSSIBLE CAUSES AND SOLUTIONS
1. **Workpiece overheating:**
   Reduce wire feed speed.
   Decrease weld current.
2. **Travel speed too slow:**
   Increase travel speed and ensure that travel speed is kept steady.
3. **Excessive material at weld:**
   Reduce wire feed speed.

### POSSIBLE CAUSES AND SOLUTIONS
4. **Incorrect welding technique:**
   Maintain 1/2" or less CTWD.
   Keep arc on leading edge of weld puddle.
   Hold MIG Gun at proper angles.
5. **Insufficient weld heat:**
   Reduce travel speed.
   Increase weld current.
6. **Workpieces too thick/close:**
   Bevel thick workpieces, allow slight gap, and weld in several passes.
7. **Insufficient weld material:**
   Increase wire feed speed.

2. **Excessive heat:**
   Weld a small portion and allow to cool before proceeding.
   Increase travel speed.

## Coat of Slag Over Weld

[FIGURE slag-coated-weld-top-view | bbox: 175,1610,690,1780 | Top view of a weld bead partially chipped away to reveal the weld underneath the slag coating, illustrating what slag looks like on a flux-cored wire weld.]
- Label at left: "TOP VIEW"
- Drawing: an elongated dark weld bead with a rippled/scaled texture (slag) running along a top-view seam, with a torch/gun nozzle depicted at the right end feeding into the bead, and a lighter round area at the right end showing the weld puddle/tip where slag has been chipped away.
- Caption below drawing: "PARTIALLY CHIPPED AWAY TO SHOW WELD"

Slag is a necessary part of a flux-cored wire weld. It shields the weld from impurities. Clean off the slag with a Chipping Hammer and Wire Brush after welding.

Gas-shielded MIG welds are protected by the shielding gas and do not need slag to protect them.

## Weld Not Adhering Properly

Gaps present between weld and previous bead or between weld and workpiece. See areas below.

[FIGURE weld-not-adhering-profile | bbox: 1015,1300,1520,1490 | Profile view of a weld cross-section with dashed circles highlighting gap locations where the weld has not properly adhered to the previous bead or workpiece.]
- Label at left: "PROFILE VIEW"
- Drawing: two gray rectangular workpiece halves with a dark teardrop-shaped weld bead at the joint; three dashed white circles overlay the drawing — one at the upper-left edge of the bead, one at the upper-right edge of the bead, and one at the bottom tip of the bead where it meets the underside of the joint — marking areas where gaps commonly form between the weld and the base metal or previous bead.

### POSSIBLE CAUSES AND SOLUTIONS
1. **Incorrect welding technique:**
   Place stringer bead at correct place in joint.
   Adjust workpiece position or weld angle to permit proper welding to bottom of piece.
   Pause briefly at sides during weave bead.
   Keep arc on leading edge of weld puddle.
   Hold MIG Gun at proper angles.
2. **Insufficient weld heat:**
   Increase current.
   Increase wire feed speed.
3. **Dirty workpiece:**
   Clean workpiece down to bare metal.
4. **Insufficient weld material:**
   Increase wire feed speed.
5. **Workpiece gap too narrow:**
   Widen groove or increase bevel.

## Wire Weld−Porosity

**Small cavities or holes in the bead.**

[FIGURE wire-weld-porosity | bbox: 88,220,624,360 | Top view illustration of a weld bead showing small white circular voids scattered along the length of the bead, illustrating porosity defects.]
- Label: "TOP VIEW" to the left of the illustration
- Illustration: a horizontal weld bead made of overlapping scalloped ripples, ending in a rounded weld puddle on the right; small white speckles (cavities/holes) are scattered throughout the bead surface, especially concentrated toward the right (puddle) end

#### POSSIBLE CAUSES AND SOLUTIONS

1. **Incorrect polarity:**
   Check that polarity is set correctly for type of welding.

2. **Insufficient shielding gas (MIG only):**
   Increase flow of gas.
   Clean nozzle.
   Maintain proper CTWD.

3. **Incorrect shielding gas (MIG only):**
   Use shielding gas recommended by wire supplier.

4. **Dirty workpiece or welding wire:**
   Clean workpiece down to bare metal.
   Make certain that wire is clean and free from oil, coatings, and other residues.

5. **Inconsistent travel speed:**
   Maintain steady travel speed.

6. **CTWD too long:**
   Reduce CTWD.

**Fine spatter is normal.**
**Spatter that is grainy and large is a problem.**

#### POSSIBLE CAUSES AND SOLUTIONS

1. **Dirty workpiece or welding wire:**
   Clean workpiece down to bare metal.
   Make certain that wire is clean and free from oil, coatings, and other residues.

2. **Incorrect polarity:**
   Check that polarity is set correctly for type of welding.

3. **Insufficient shielding gas (MIG only):**
   Increase flow of gas.
   Clean nozzle.
   Maintain proper CTWD.

4. **Wire feeding too fast:**
   Reduce wire feed speed.

5. **CTWD too long:**
   Reduce CTWD.

## Wire Weld−Crooked/Wavy Bead

## Wire Weld−Burn-Through

**Base material melts away,**
**leaving a hole in the weld.**

[FIGURE wire-weld-burn-through | bbox: 838,1378,1373,1523 | Top view illustration of a weld bead with a large irregular white hole burned through the base material, illustrating a burn-through defect.]

1. **Workpiece overheating:**
   Reduce current and/or wire feed speed.

2. **Travel speed too slow:**
   Increase travel speed and ensure that travel speed is kept steady.

3. **Excessive material at weld:**
   Reduce wire feed speed.
